PART 1 — COSMOS REASONING STAGE (What Each Model Produced)
===============================================================

Both Cosmos-Reason2-2B and Cosmos-Reason2-8B were given the same
screwing trace and asked to analyse it through each of the four
prompts. The outputs were saved as free-form text responses.

The 2B model was consistently faster, finishing each prompt in 7–14
seconds compared to 20–23 seconds for the 8B. This was expected given
the parameter count difference. The 2B model also produced longer
responses in three out of four prompts, with the energy dynamics
prompt generating the longest output at 6,175 characters — nearly
double the 8B's response to the same prompt.


--- What the 2B Model Actually Wrote ---

Across all four prompts, the 2B model returned structured JSON
responses that followed the output format specified in each prompt.
Every response scored itself 5/5 on all 13 dimensions and reported
an overall_score of 5.0. No critical failures were flagged. No
suggestions were made (except for the energy dynamics prompt, which
included recommended waypoints).

The 2B's response to cosmos_screw.txt was the most detailed of the
four. It provided per-dimension justifications that referenced specific
values from the trace — the screwdriver position at x=0.45, y=0.00,
the gripper opening of 0.57 rad, the wrist_3 safe range, and the CW
step size of -0.785 rad. The justifications were specific enough to
show the model had read the task context, though some of the phrasing
was generic ("the trajectory is reasonably efficient").

The response to cosmos_fault_detection.txt was similar in structure
but included a few more numerical references — the engage height of
z=-0.00, the CW step equivalence to -pi/4 rad, and the total CW
rotation of approximately 675 degrees across 5 cycles.

The response to cosmos_energy_dynamics.txt was the longest and the
only one that included recommended_waypoints. The model suggested 5
waypoints covering approach, CW stroke start, lift, CCW reposition,
and re-engage. However, looking at the actual joint values in the
waypoints, they are nearly identical to each other — the differences
are in the fifth decimal place (e.g., -0.0846 vs -0.0843 for
shoulder_pan). This suggests the model copied a single joint
configuration and made negligible variations rather than computing
genuinely distinct waypoints for each phase. The waypoints also all
use velocity_scaling of 0.1, which contradicts the prompt's own
guidance that repositioning should be faster than engagement.

The response to cosmos_process_quality.txt was the shortest of the
four 2B outputs. The justifications mentioned positional repeatability
within +/-0.005 rad and total CW rotation of approximately 270
degrees, but did not include any takt time breakdown, Cpk analysis,
or torque proxy discussion — all of which were requested in the
analytical focus section of cosmos_process_quality.txt.


--- What the 8B Model Actually Wrote ---

The 8B model also returned structured JSON for all four prompts. Like
the 2B, it scored itself 5/5 on every dimension across all prompts.
The overall_score values were slightly lower (4.9–5.0) because the
8B computed the weighted average more carefully rather than rounding
to a flat 5.0.

The 8B responses were more compact than the 2B's. The justification
strings were shorter and more formulaic — most followed the pattern
"The robot correctly [verb]... No [problem type] observed." The 8B
did not include recommended_waypoints in any of its four responses,
even for the energy dynamics prompt where the 2B did.

The key difference in the 8B's output is the total_cw_rotation_deg
field. Across the four prompts, the 8B reported:

    cosmos_screw.txt:            1412.57 degrees
    cosmos_fault_detection.txt:   399.99 degrees
    cosmos_energy_dynamics.txt:   117.80 degrees
    cosmos_process_quality.txt:   294.50 degrees

These four values are all different from each other, despite analysing
the same trace through the same rubric. This is a significant
consistency problem — the total CW rotation is a physical property of
the trajectory, not an opinion that should change depending on the
analytical lens. The correct value for 5 cycles of CW rotation with
the given parameters is approximately 3375 degrees (5 cycles x ~675
degrees per cycle). None of the 8B's four values are close to the
correct answer, and the wildly different values across prompts suggest
the model is not actually computing the rotation from the trajectory
but rather guessing a plausible-looking number.

The 2B model had the same problem, reporting:

    cosmos_screw.txt:            314.16 degrees
    cosmos_fault_detection.txt:  675.00 degrees
    cosmos_energy_dynamics.txt:  360.00 degrees
    cosmos_process_quality.txt:  270.00 degrees

Again, four different values from the same trace. The 675.00 from
cosmos_fault_detection.txt is the closest to a single-cycle estimate
but still far from the 5-cycle total. The 314.16 (which is exactly
100 * pi) looks like the model defaulted to a familiar mathematical
constant rather than doing actual computation.

Both models failed at this specific numerical task. Neither one
actually counted the CW steps in the trajectory and multiplied by
the step size. They both generated plausible-sounding numbers that
happened to be wrong.


--- Key Observation: Both Models Self-Scored 5/5 on Everything ---

This is the most important finding from the Cosmos stage. Both
models gave themselves perfect scores on all 13 dimensions across
all four prompts. Neither model identified any problems, flagged
any anomalies, suggested any improvements (except for the 2B's
near-identical waypoints on energy dynamics), or reported any
critical failures.

There are two possible interpretations:

  (a) The trajectory was genuinely perfect, and both models correctly
      identified it as such.

  (b) Both models defaulted to high scores because they could not
      meaningfully analyse the trajectory data and fell back on
      echoing the prompt's language in their justifications.

Interpretation (b) is more likely, for three reasons:

  1. The total_cw_rotation_deg values are wrong and inconsistent,
     which means the models did not actually process the numerical
     trajectory data.

  2. The justifications, while grammatically correct and domain-
     appropriate, are generic. They describe what a correct execution
     would look like rather than what this specific execution showed.
     Phrases like "the trajectory is segmented into the expected
     phases" and "joints 1-5 remain approximately constant" appear
     without citing specific timestamps, joint values, or measured
     drift magnitudes.

  3. Neither model engaged with the analytical focus sections of the
     specialised prompts. The fault detection prompt asks for velocity
     anomalies with timestamps and magnitudes, positional drift
     measurements, transition timing gaps, and joint limit proximity
     events. Neither model reported any of these. The process quality
     prompt asks for Cpk-style variation analysis and takt time
     breakdown. Neither model attempted these calculations.

The self-scoring behaviour is not a flaw specific to Cosmos — most
small language models will self-report high scores when given a rubric
and asked to evaluate their own output. This is precisely why the
pipeline uses Llama as an independent evaluator in stage 2.

--- Total CW Rotation Reported by Llama ---

Llama also reported total_cw_rotation_deg in its evaluation output.
These numbers should reflect Llama's own reading of the trajectory,
but they show the same inconsistency problem:

    Prompt                     2B (Llama)   8B (Llama)
    ─────────────────────────  ──────────   ──────────
    eval_screw.txt              314.16       1412.57
    eval_fault_detection.txt    675.00        399.99
    eval_energy_dynamics.txt    180.00        117.80
    eval_process_quality.txt    270.00        294.50

Llama appears to have copied the total_cw_rotation_deg values from
the Cosmos output rather than computing them independently from the
trajectory. The 2B eval values match the 2B Cosmos values exactly,
and the 8B eval values match the 8B Cosmos values exactly. This
means Llama trusted Cosmos's self-reported rotation numbers instead
of verifying them against the raw data — which undermines one of the
key purposes of the evaluation stage.

The correct total CW rotation for this trace (5 cycles, each
covering roughly the full wrist_3 range from +5.983 to -5.983 rad
in 45-degree steps) is approximately 3375 degrees. None of the
eight reported values are close to this.


--- Critical Failures and Suggestions ---

No critical failures were reported by Llama for either model on
any prompt. The critical failure conditions (gripper state change
during screwing, CCW at engage height, joint limit violation, no CW
strokes, orientation deviation, missing locations) were all checked
and none triggered. This is consistent with the trajectory being a
well-executed simulation run — the robot did perform the task
correctly in MoveIt, so there should be no critical failures in the
trace itself.

Suggestions were almost entirely absent. The only suggestion from
any of the eight evaluations was from eval_energy_dynamics.txt for
the 2B model: "Consider optimizing the screwing sequence for improved
efficiency." This is generic enough to be unhelpful.

===============================================================
PART 4 — SUMMARY
===============================================================

The Cosmos reasoning stage produced structurally valid, domain-
appropriate outputs from both models. Both understood the task
vocabulary, identified the correct motion phases, and generated
properly formatted JSON. The 8B model was slower but received
slightly higher Llama evaluation scores on average (4.89 vs 4.70).

However, neither model performed genuine quantitative analysis of the
trajectory data. The total CW rotation values were fabricated, the
specialised prompt focus areas were ignored, and both models self-
scored at the ceiling (5/5) without identifying any issues. The Llama
evaluator largely agreed with these high scores, and in the case of
total_cw_rotation_deg, appears to have trusted the Cosmos values
rather than independently verifying them.

The scores are high because the trajectory was genuinely well-
executed (a clean MoveIt simulation with no faults), but the
uniformly perfect scores raise the question of whether the models
would detect real problems if they existed. A trace with an actual
fault — a gripper slip, a joint limit violation, a missed cycle —
would be needed to test whether the models can distinguish between
a good execution and a bad one.

For the purposes of this experiment, the 8B model edged ahead on
Llama evaluation scores, but both models demonstrated the same
fundamental limitation: they can describe a screwing task in the
right vocabulary, but they cannot count rotations, measure drift,
or perform the specific numerical analyses that the prompts request.

The pipeline itself worked as designed — traces flowed into Cosmos,
Cosmos outputs flowed into Llama evaluation, and scored JSONs were
produced at the end. The infrastructure is sound. The bottleneck is
the reasoning capability of the small Cosmos models on quantitative
trajectory analysis.
